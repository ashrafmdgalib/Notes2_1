\section{Semiconductor Materials}
\textbf{Conductor: }The term conductor is used for any material that will support generous flow of charge when the voltage is applied across its terminals. Ex:Cu,Al etc. \\ [3pt]
\textbf{Insulator: }An insulator is a material that offers very low conductivity when voltage is applied. Ex : Mica,Rubber,Wood etc. \\ [3pt]
\textbf{Semiconductor: }It is a material that has conductivity more than insulators but less than the conductor. Ex: \ce{Si, Ge} \\ [10pt]
\textbf{\large Resistivity and Conductivity}
$$\rho = \dfrac{1}{\sigma} = \dfrac{RA}{L}$$
\hspace*{5cm}Where, 
\begin{adjustwidth}{6.5cm}{0pt}
    $\rho$ = Resistivity\,\,\,\,[Its a property of material] \\
    $\sigma$ = Conductivity \\
    A = Area of the cross-section\\
    R = Resistance \\ 
    L = Length 
\end{adjustwidth}
\vspace*{10pt}
According to Ohm's Law: $R\uparrow I\downarrow \quad \text{and} \quad R\downarrow I\uparrow$ \\ [3pt]
\hspace*{4cm}So, 
\vspace*{-10pt}
\begin{align*}
    \rho_c < \rho_s < \rho _i \qquad &\rho \longrightarrow \text{Resistivity} \\
    \sigma_c > \sigma_s > \sigma _i  \qquad &\sigma \longrightarrow \text{Conductivity} \\
    I_c > I_s > I_i \qquad &I \longrightarrow \text{Current }
\end{align*}

    \item Silicon crystallize in a diamond cubic lattice. Each \ce{Si} has 4 valence electrons. To achieve stability, each atom shares 1 valence electron with each of its four neighbours.

\textbf{\large Intrinsic Semiconductor}
\begin{itemize}
    \item They are the pure semiconductors. No other atoms included. 
    \item Free electrons are only due to natural causes. 
    \item  At $ T=0K$ temperature, all bonds are intact, with no free
electrons for conduction.
    \item At $T\neq 0K$,some covalent bonds  break where a broken covalent bond frees
an electron, allowing it to move and conduct
current when an electric field is applied.
\end{itemize}
\begin{questionbox}[Why do we bother with the concept of the hole?]
    We bother with holes because they simplify the description of current flow in semiconductors. Instead of tracking billions of electrons in the valence band, we treat the absence of one electron as a positive charge carrier. This makes equations (like drift current, diffusion current, pn-junction equations, etc.) symmetric and elegant.Device physics depends on holes (like PN junctions (diodes), BJTs) many modern devices rely on the interaction of electrons and holes.
$$\text{Current in a diode} = \text{electrons injected one way} + \text{holes injected the other way}$$
Even though holes are “not real,” they behave as if they are real positive particles.
They have measurable mobility, effective mass, and contribute to measurable current density.
Hall-effect experiments even let us measure whether the dominant carriers are electrons or holes (n-type vs. p-type).
\\ So holes are not imaginary $-$ they are a useful quasi-particle.
\end{questionbox}
\vspace{1em}
\begin{questionbox}[ Why are holes slower than electron?]
    Holes are slower than electrons because they have a larger effective mass and experience more scattering in the valence band.Electrons occupy the conduction band, which has a simpler, parabolic energy-momentum relation.The E$-$k relation (energy vs. crystal momentum):
    \[
    \frac{1}{m^*}=\frac{1}{\hbar ^2}\dfrac{dE^2}{dk^2}
    \]
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If the band is very curved (steep parabola) $\rightarrow \dfrac{dE^2}{dk^2}$
 is large $\rightarrow$ small effective mass.
 \item If the band is flatter (gentle parabola)   $\rightarrow \dfrac{dE^2}{dk^2}$ is small $\rightarrow$ large effective mass.
    \end{itemize}
    For $e^-$ effective mass is small $\rightarrow$ they accelerate easily.

On the other hand holes are in the valence band, which is more complex (split into heavy-hole and light-hole bands). The average effective mass of holes is larger. Bigger effective mass $\rightarrow$ lower mobility.Again holes interact more strongly with the crystal lattice because of their complex band structure.

This increases collisions (phonon and impurity scattering), reducing their mobility further. %This gives them lower mobility compared to electrons in the conduction band.
\end{questionbox}

\section{Mass Action Law}
\textbf{Statement: }Under thermal equilibrium, the product of the free electrons concentration and the free hole concentration is equal to a constant to the square of intrinsic carrier concentration.\\
In Intrinsic Semiconductor, \\
\hspace*{4cm}no of $e^-$ = no of holes\\
\hspace*{4cm}$n = p = n_i$
\begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{c|l}
        \multirow{3}{*}{$n.p={n_i}^2$} \quad &  \quad $n = $ Free electron concentration \\ 
        & \quad $p =$ Free hole concentration \\ 
        & \quad $n_i =$ Intrinsic Carrier Concentration
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\underline{Prove :}\\
 Because of thermal energy ,the $e^-$ will break the covalent bond and participate in conduction leaving the hole behind and generate charge carriers.
 Under the thermal equilibrium.\\
 \hspace*{3cm}Rate of Generation = Rate of Recombination\\
 $$G=R$$
 
 \[R\propto n \]
 \[R\propto p \]
\[R=rnp \text{\hspace*{1cm} [r $\rightarrow$ Recombination constant]}\] 
In intrinsic semiconductor, $G=rn_i^2$. Dopping in intrinsic semiconductor is not affected by the generation of holes or electrons.

\section{Conductivity of Semiconductor}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Metals are unipolar. But semiconductors are bipolar. 
    \item When Electric Field E is applied, $e^-$ and holes are drifted in opposite direction bu the current due to them will be in the same direction. 
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}\hspace*{1.5cm}
    \begin{minipage}{0.25\linewidth}
        \hspace*{-10pt}$\begin{aligned}[t]
            &v_d \propto E \\
            \Rightarrow\,\, &v_d = \mu E 
        \end{aligned}$ \\ [8pt]
        $i = neAv_d$ \\ [8pt] $\begin{aligned}[t]
            \sigma &= ne\mu \\ 
            &= (n\mu_n+p\mu_p)e
        \end{aligned}$ \\ [8pt]
        $\begin{aligned}
            J &= \dfrac{i}{A} \\ 
            &= nev_d \\
            &= ne\mu E \\ 
            &= \sigma E \\ 
            &= (n\mu_n+p\mu_p)eE 
        \end{aligned}$
    \end{minipage}
    \begin{minipage}{0.05\linewidth}
        \rule{1pt}{7.2cm}
    \end{minipage}
    \begin{minipage}{0.3\linewidth}
        $\mu = $ Mobility \\
        $i = $ Current \\ 
        $n = $ no. of charge carrier \\ 
        $A = $ Area of cross-section \\
        $J = $ Current Density \\ 
        $\sigma = $ Conductivity \\
        $E = $ Electric Field \\ 
        $v_d = $ Drift Velocity \\ 
        $e = $ Charge of a $e^-$ \\ 
        $e.m = $ Effective Mass 
        $$\mu \propto \dfrac{1}{e.m}$$
        $$e.m.\,\, of \,\, hole >> e.m. \,\,of \,\,e^- \qquad \mu_p << \mu_n$$

    \end{minipage}
\end{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, title={\textbf{Example 1.1}: The mobilities of free electron and holes in a pure germanium are 0.38 and 0.18 $m^2/V.s$. Find the value of intrinsic conductivity. Assume $n_i = 2.5\times 10^{19}/m^3$ at room temperature.}]
    Given,
\begin{adjustwidth}{1cm}{3pt}
    Mobility of free electron, $\mu_n = 0.38/m^3$ \\ 
    Mobility of holes, $\mu_p = 0.18/m^3$
\end{adjustwidth}
$\because $This is a pure germanium semiconductor.
\vspace*{-5pt}
$$\therefore n = p = n_i = 2.5\times 10^{19}/m^3$$
We know that,
\vspace*{-5pt}
\begin{align*}
    \text{Conductivity},\, \sigma &= (n\mu_n+0\mu_p)e \\ 
    &= (0.38+0.18)\times 2.5\times 10^{19}\times 1.6\times10^{-19} (\Omega-m)^{-1} \\ 
    &= 2.24\,\,(\Omega-m)^{-1} \, _{(ans)}
\end{align*}
\end{tcolorbox}
\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, title = {\textbf{Question 1.1: }In a germanium sample, a donor type impurity is added to the extent of 1 atom per $10^8$ germanium atoms. Show that resistivity of the germanium sample drops to 3.7 ohm-cm  \\ 
    Given, 
    \begin{adjustwidth}{1cm}{5pt}
        \begin{tabular}{ll}
            $\mu_n=3800cm^2/V.s$ & \hspace*{20pt}$n_i = 2.5\times 10^{13}/cm^3$ \\
            $\mu_p = 1800cm^2/V.s$ &\hspace*{16pt} $N_{ge}=4.41 \times 10^{22}/cm^3$ 
        \end{tabular}
    \end{adjustwidth} }]
    Given,\\
    \begin{adjustwidth}{1cm}{3pt}
Mobility of free electron: $\mu_n=3800\,\text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}$ \par
Mobility of holes: $\mu_p=1800\,\text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}$ \par
Concentration of intrinsic charge carrier: $n_i=2.5\times10^{13}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ \par
No of Germanium $e^-$ per atom: $N_{Ge}=4.41\times10^{22}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ 
\end{adjustwidth}

For doping semiconductor,
\[
N_D=\frac{N_{Ge}}{\text{Extent electron per atom}}
=\frac{4.41\times 10^{22}}{10^{8}}
=4.41\times 10^{14}\,\text{cm}^{-3}
\]


$n=N_D=4.41\times 10^{14}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$


\[
p=\frac{n_i^2}{n}
=\frac{(2.5\times10^{13})^2}{4.41\times 10^{14}}
=1.51\times10^{12}\,\text{cm}^{-3}
\]

We know,
\[
\rho=\frac{1}{\sigma}
=\frac{1}{(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)e}
\]

\[
=\frac{1}{\left(4.41\times 10^{14}\times3800
+1.51\times 10^{12}\times1800\right)\times 1.6\times10^{-19}}
\]

\[
=3.723\,\Omega\cdot\text{cm}
\]

\end{tcolorbox}

Barrier Potential/ Built-in Potential,
\begin{align*}
    V_b &= \dfrac{kT}{e}\ln \left(\dfrac{N_AN_D}{{n_i}^2}\right) \\ 
    &= V_T\ln \left(\dfrac{N_AN_D}{{n_i}^2}\right) 
\end{align*}
Here,
\begin{adjustwidth}{1.5cm}{0pt}
    $k = $ Boltzman constant = $1.38066\times 10^{-23}J/K$ \\ 
    T = Absolute Temperature \\
    $e = $ Charge of one $e^- = 1.6\times 10^{-19}C$ 
\end{adjustwidth}
\vspace*{5pt}
\textbf{At room temperature (300K/$27^\circ $C):}
\begin{align*}
    V_T &= \dfrac{kT}{e} \\
    &= \dfrac{1.38066\times 10^{-23}\times 300}{1.6\times 10^{-19}} \\ 
    &= 0.026 V
\end{align*}
$\bullet\,\,\, $In ideal cases, we consider the barrier potential for \ce{Si} is 0.7V and 0.3V is for \ce{Ge} at room temperature.
\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, title = {\textbf{Example 1.2: }Consider a pn junction at room temperature, doped ar $N_A=10^{16}/cm^3$ in the p-region and $N_D=10^{17}/cm^3$ in the n-region. Intrinsic carrier density is $1.5/cm^3$ at room temperature. Calculate the built-in potential. }]
    Given,
    \begin{adjustwidth}{1cm}{5pt}
        Acceptor ions Concentration, $N_A =10^{16}/cm^3$ \\
        Donor ions Concentration, $N_D=10^{17}/cm^3$ \\
        At room temperature, $V_T = 0.026 V$ \\ 
        Intrinsic Charge density, $n_i = 1.5/cm^3$
    \end{adjustwidth}
    We know that,
    \begin{align*}
        \text{Built-in Potential, }V_b &= V_T\ln\left(\dfrac{N_AN_D}{{n_i}^2}\right) \\ 
        &=0.026\, \ln\left\{\dfrac{10^{16}\times 10^{17}}{(1.5\times 10^{10})^2}\right\} \\ 
        &= 0.757 V
    \end{align*}
\end{tcolorbox}

Width of Depletion Region,
\begin{align*}
    W_d = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\varepsilon}{e}\left(\dfrac{1}{N_A}+\dfrac{1}{N_D}\right)V_b}
\end{align*}
\begin{center}\hspace*{3.3cm}
    \begin{tabular}{c|l}
        \multirow{3}{*}{$\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$} & $\varepsilon = $ Electric Permittivity \\
        & $\varepsilon_r = $ Relative Permittivity \\
        & $\varepsilon_0 = $ Permittivity of Vacuum 
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
    \item For \ce{Si}\begin{itemize}\vspace*{-7pt}
        \item[*] $\varepsilon = 1.04\times 10^{-12}F/cm$
        \item[*] $0.1\mu m < W_d < 1\mu m$
    \end{itemize}
    \item For \ce{Ge}, $\varepsilon = 1.12448\times 10^{-12}F/cm$
\end{itemize}
\vspace*{8pt}
\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, title={\textbf{Example 1.3: }Consider a silicon pn-junction at room temperature doped at $N_A=10^{16}/cm^3$ in the p-region and $N_D=10^{17}/cm^3$ in the n-region. Intrinsic carrier density is $1.5/cm^3$ at room temperature. Calculate the width of depletion region.}]
    Given,
    \begin{adjustwidth}{1cm}{5pt}
        Acceptor ions Concentration, $N_A =10^{16}/cm^3$ \\
        Donor ions Concentration, $N_D=10^{17}/cm^3$ \\
        At room temperature, $V_T = 0.026 V$ \\ 
        Intrinsic Charge density, $n_i = 1.5/cm^3$ \\
        Permittivity of \ce{Si}, $\varepsilon = 1.04\times 10^{-12}$ F/cm 
    \end{adjustwidth}
    We know that,
    \begin{align*}
        \text{Built-in Potential, }V_b &= V_T\ln\left(\dfrac{N_AN_D}{{n_i}^2}\right) \\ 
        &=0.026\, \ln\left\{\dfrac{10^{16}\times 10^{17}}{(1.5\times 10^{10})^2}\right\} \\ 
        &= 0.757 V
    \end{align*}
    \begin{align*}
        \text{Width of depletion region,} W_d &= \sqrt{\dfrac{2\varepsilon}{e}\left(\dfrac{1}{N_A}+\dfrac{1}{N_D}\right).V_b} \\ 
        &= \sqrt{\dfrac{2\times 1.04\times 10^{-12}}{1.6\times 10^{-19}}\left(\dfrac{1}{10^{16}}+\dfrac{1}{10^{17}}\right)\times 0.757} \\
        &= 3.290\times 10^{-5}\text{cm} \\ 
        &= 0.3290 \mu\text{m}
    \end{align*}
\end{tcolorbox}
